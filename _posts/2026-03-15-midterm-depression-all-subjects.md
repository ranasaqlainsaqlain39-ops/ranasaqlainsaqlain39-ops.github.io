Nobody warns you about midterm season in your first semester of university. Not really. People say it will be busy, that you should prepare early, that time management matters. But until you are actually sitting in the middle of it — surrounded by notes you barely understand, assignment deadlines stacking up faster than you can process them, and an exam schedule that seems designed to break you — you do not truly grasp what busy means at the university level. I learned this the hard way during my first semester at UET Faisalabad, and the experience left a mark that changed how I study forever.

The weeks leading up to midterms started normally enough. Classes were running, labs were happening, and I was keeping up — or at least I thought I was. The problem was that keeping up with daily lectures is not the same as understanding material deeply enough to reproduce it under exam pressure. I had been attending classes, taking notes, and moving on. What I had not been doing was reviewing, practicing, or connecting concepts across weeks. When midterm season arrived, I discovered just how large the gap between those two things really was.

## The Weight of Multiple Subjects Simultaneously

What made first semester midterms particularly brutal was the sheer number of subjects converging at the same time. ASIC, Applied Physics, and other core courses all demanded attention simultaneously. Each subject had its own logic, its own vocabulary, its own way of thinking. Switching between them in a single study session felt like changing languages mid-conversation. The mental fatigue was real and cumulative — by the time I had spent two hours on one subject, I had very little left to give the next one.

Applied Physics was especially challenging because it combined mathematical problem-solving with conceptual understanding in a way that required both, not just one. You could not memorize your way through it, and you could not logic your way through it without the mathematical tools. I had left gaps in both areas during the regular semester, and midterms exposed every single one of them. Sitting in that exam hall, staring at problems I recognized but could not fully solve, was a specific kind of uncomfortable that I hope never to repeat.

ASIC brought its own set of challenges. The subject required a kind of systematic, detail-oriented thinking that I had not yet fully developed. Circuit analysis, logic operations, the precision required to get answers right — these demanded consistent practice over weeks, not cramming over nights. I had the notes. I had attended the lectures. What I did not have was enough repetition with the material to make it automatic under pressure. The difference between knowing something and knowing it well enough to use it in an exam is enormous, and midterms made that difference impossible to ignore.

## Assignments and Quizzes Piling Up

While managing exam preparation, the regular academic machinery kept running. Assignments that I had been postponing — telling myself I would finish them after the next lecture, after the weekend, after I felt less tired — suddenly had imminent deadlines. Quizzes for DLD, Database Systems, and OOP appeared on the schedule with little warning. The combination of exam preparation and regular coursework created a workload that felt genuinely unmanageable.

I remember days during that period when my classmates and I would arrive at campus early in the morning and barely have time to eat a proper meal before the day ended. We were surviving on chai, quick snacks between classes, and the shared misery of knowing that everyone around us was in the same situation. There was a strange comfort in that collective struggle — knowing that I was not uniquely failing, that this was hard for everyone — but comfort does not translate into marks, and I knew it.

The quizzes were particularly stressful because they required recall under time pressure. Database Systems quizzes tested whether you understood the concepts well enough to apply them quickly. DLD required precise logical thinking with no room for approximation. OOP demanded that you had actually practiced writing code, not just read about it. Each of these was a small exam in itself, and when they arrived in the same week as midterm preparation, the pressure compounded in ways that were difficult to manage.

## The Lesson Learned the Hard Way

What came out of that midterm season was not just a set of results but a fundamental shift in how I understood the relationship between consistent effort and performance. The students who came through midterms with confidence were not necessarily more intelligent than the rest of us. They were more consistent. They had reviewed material weekly rather than waiting for exam season. They had done problems regularly rather than saving them for the last week. They had built understanding gradually rather than trying to compress weeks of learning into days.

I made a decision after those midterms that I have tried to honor ever since: I would not let work accumulate to the point of becoming unmanageable again. That meant reviewing notes within a day or two of each lecture. It meant completing assignments when they were assigned rather than when they were due. It meant practicing problems regularly rather than reactively. These are not revolutionary study techniques — every teacher says the same things. But there is a difference between knowing good habits and actually adopting them after you have felt the consequences of ignoring them.

That first midterm season at UET Faisalabad was painful in the moment. But the clarity it produced — about how I needed to work, about what consistent effort actually looked like in practice — was worth more than a perfect score would have been. Some lessons only land when they cost you something. This one cost me sleep, comfort, and some marks. In return, it gave me a way of working that has served me far better ever since.
