Nobody warns you about midterm season in your first semester of university. Not really. People say it will be busy, that you should prepare early, that time management is important. But until you are actually sitting in the middle of it — surrounded by notes you barely understand, assignment deadlines stacking up faster than you can process them, and an exam schedule that seems designed to test not just your knowledge but your endurance — you do not truly grasp what busy means at the university level. I learned this the hard way during my first semester at UET Faisalabad, and the experience left a mark on me that changed how I approach studying permanently.

The weeks leading up to midterms started normally enough. Classes were running, labs were happening, and I was attending everything and taking notes. The problem was that attending class and taking notes is not the same as understanding material well enough to reproduce it under exam pressure. I had been moving through the semester in a kind of forward momentum — going to class, writing things down, moving on to the next day — without stopping to consolidate what I had learned, to practice applying concepts, to test myself on material before the exam forced me to. When midterm season arrived, the gap between what I had attended and what I actually understood became impossible to ignore.

What made first semester midterms particularly difficult was the sheer number of subjects demanding serious attention at the same time. ASIC, Applied Physics, Digital Logic Design — each of these had its own internal logic, its own vocabulary, its own way of thinking that took time to internalize. Switching between them in a single study session felt like changing languages mid-conversation. The mental energy required to shift between circuit analysis and physics problems and logical operations was real and cumulative. By the time I had spent a couple of hours on one subject, I had noticeably less capacity for the next one. I was managing fatigue as much as I was managing content, and I had not planned for that.

Applied Physics was the subject that gave me the most trouble during that period. It combined mathematical problem-solving with conceptual understanding in a way that required both simultaneously — you could not reason your way through a problem without the mathematical tools, and the mathematical tools were meaningless without the conceptual framework they operated within. I had allowed gaps to form in both areas during the regular weeks of the semester. Physics lectures that I had attended but not fully processed, problem sets I had completed well enough to submit without understanding why the methods worked. Midterms exposed every one of those gaps at once, and sitting in the exam hall staring at problems I recognized but could not fully execute was an experience I found genuinely difficult.

ASIC brought its own specific challenges. The subject demanded a kind of precise, systematic thinking that I had not yet fully developed. Circuit problems do not tolerate approximation — the answer is either correct or it is not, and getting to the correct answer requires following the right procedure all the way through without losing track of any step. I had the theory in my notes. I had seen the methods demonstrated in lectures. What I had not done was practice them enough times for them to feel automatic. Under exam pressure, methods that I had encountered but not internalized required conscious effort to recall and apply, and that effort competed with the time pressure of the exam itself.

While all of this was happening, the regular semester machinery kept running. Assignments that I had been telling myself I would complete properly once things settled down suddenly had imminent deadlines. Quizzes for subjects I had not prepared for appeared on the schedule. I remember arriving at campus early in the morning during that period and barely finding time to eat a proper meal before the day ended. My classmates were in the same situation, and there was a particular kind of shared exhaustion in those weeks that I have not experienced quite the same way since. We were all running on urgency and chai and the hope that the exams would somehow go better than our preparation warranted.

What came out of that midterm season — beyond the results themselves, which were mixed — was a shift in how I understood the relationship between consistent effort and performance under pressure. The students who moved through midterms with relative confidence were not necessarily more talented than the rest of us. They were more consistent. They had been reviewing material after each lecture rather than letting it accumulate. They had been practicing problems throughout the semester rather than encountering them for the first time during exam preparation. They had built understanding gradually and repeatedly rather than trying to compress weeks of learning into a few intense days. The difference in outcome was a direct reflection of the difference in process, and seeing that clearly was sobering.

I made a decision after those midterms that I have tried to keep ever since. I would not let confusion accumulate. When something in a lecture did not make sense, I would address it the same day — by asking a classmate, by working through additional examples, by sitting with the confusion until it resolved rather than writing it down and moving on. I would complete assignments when they were assigned rather than when they were due. I would review notes within a day or two of each class rather than only before exams. These habits are not original or sophisticated — every teacher and every study guide recommends something like them. But there is a meaningful difference between knowing that consistent effort matters and having felt what inconsistency costs. That first midterm season at UET gave me the felt version of that lesson, and it has stayed with me.
